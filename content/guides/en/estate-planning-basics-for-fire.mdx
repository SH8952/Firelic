Someone who retires at 65 usually has decades of paperwork habits already in place: a will drafted after the kids were born, beneficiary forms updated at every job change, maybe a conversation with a lawyer once income stabilized. Someone who reaches FIRE at 38 often has none of that, not because they're careless, but because most of the financial industry assumes estate planning is a task for people much closer to the end of their working life. That assumption breaks down for early retirees, who may have forty or fifty years of remaining life expectancy, a portfolio that's already substantial, and, in many cases, young children who would need a guardian named and a trust structured if something happened unexpectedly. The math of FIRE is about accumulating enough; estate planning is about making sure that accumulation actually reaches the people it's meant for, in the way you intended, without a probate court or a decade-old beneficiary form deciding for you.

## Why this matters more, not less, once you've hit your number

A working assumption behind traditional retirement planning is that estate documents can wait until net worth is meaningfully established and life is "settled." FIRE inverts both halves of that assumption. Net worth is often already at its highest relative level early in retirement, before decades of withdrawals draw it down, and life is anything but settled: an early retiree might be traveling internationally, changing addresses across state or country lines, or entering a new relationship years after documents were last touched. A retiree who reached FIRE at 40 with a $1.4 million portfolio and no updated will is, in practical terms, sitting on more unprotected wealth than the median person their document oversight would apply to at any other stage of life. The gap between "I have enough money to retire" and "my family is protected if I'm gone tomorrow" is exactly what estate planning closes, and it's a gap that grows, not shrinks, the earlier you retire.

## The core documents, and why each one solves a specific failure mode

A will directs who receives assets that don't already have a named beneficiary, and without one, a probate court applies a default formula that rarely matches what someone would have actually chosen, especially for unmarried partners or blended families. A durable power of attorney names someone to manage your finances if you're incapacitated but still alive, which matters because a spouse or adult child otherwise has no automatic legal authority to access accounts or pay bills on your behalf. A healthcare directive, sometimes called a living will, states your medical wishes and names someone to make decisions if you can't, removing an agonizing guessing game from family members during an already difficult moment. None of these are exotic instruments reserved for the wealthy; they're closer to insurance policies that sit unused for years and then matter enormously in the one scenario where they're needed.

## A worked example: a 42-year-old FIRE retiree with two kids

Consider someone who retired at 42 with a $1.5 million portfolio, a spouse, and two children under ten. Their retirement accounts and life insurance policy together make up roughly $600,000 of that total, and those assets pass by beneficiary designation, not by will, regardless of what any will actually says. The remaining $900,000 sits in a taxable brokerage account and a home, both of which do pass through the will (or avoid probate entirely if held in a properly funded trust). If this person named their college roommate as a life insurance beneficiary back in their twenties and never updated it, that $300,000 policy goes to the roommate, not the spouse or kids, no matter what a will written decades later says. This is the single most common and most avoidable estate planning failure, and it costs nothing to fix: it just requires logging into each account and checking who's listed.

## The beneficiary designation trap, and why it's easy to fall into

Retirement accounts, life insurance, and even some bank accounts let you name a beneficiary directly on the account, and that designation legally overrides whatever a will says, every time. The trap is that these forms get filled out once, often at account opening, and then never revisited through a decade or two of job changes, marriages, divorces, and new children. For a FIRE saver who opened five or six different retirement and brokerage accounts across several employers before consolidating everything into an early retirement portfolio, there can easily be two or three old accounts with stale beneficiary information sitting somewhere, forgotten but still legally binding. A useful exercise is to list every account that holds meaningful assets, pull up its current beneficiary designation, and compare it against what you'd actually want today; for most people this takes under an hour and closes a gap that a will alone cannot.

## Cross-border complications for FIRE retirees who relocate

A meaningful share of the FIRE community moves, sometimes for cost of living, sometimes for lifestyle, and a move across a national border can put estate assets under more than one country's inheritance rules at once. Which country's law actually governs a given asset, and how it interacts with wills or beneficiary forms drafted under a different country's legal system, varies enormously and is not something to guess at from general reading. Someone who holds a brokerage account in one country, a home in another, and citizenship or tax residency in a third should generally treat this as a question for a qualified estate or tax professional in each relevant jurisdiction, rather than assuming a single will covers everything cleanly. The cost of that consultation is small compared to the risk of assets getting tied up in a foreign probate process, or taxed in ways that weren't anticipated, at a moment when the family involved is already dealing with loss.

## Common mistakes early retirees make with estate planning

The most frequent mistake is treating estate planning as a one-time task completed years ago and never revisited, when major life events (a new child, a move, a divorce, a large asset sale) should each trigger a review. A second common mistake is assuming a will alone is sufficient, when beneficiary-designated accounts, as shown above, will simply bypass it entirely if the form is out of date. A third is naming a guardian for minor children only informally, in conversation, rather than in a legal document, which leaves the decision open to a court in the exact circumstance where clarity matters most. None of these mistakes require large amounts of money or time to fix; they mostly require sitting down once, making a checklist of every account and document, and updating what's stale.

## Putting this alongside your FIRE Calculator numbers

Estate planning doesn't change the FIRE number the calculator above produces, but it changes what that number actually protects. After you've run your current age, savings rate, and target expenses through the calculator to see your projected portfolio size, it's worth treating that same portfolio size as the number you're protecting with documents, not just accumulating through savings. A retiree with a validated FIRE plan and updated beneficiary forms has actually finished the job; one with the same portfolio and decade-old paperwork has only finished half of it, and the missing half is the part that matters most for the people the plan was built to support in the first place.
