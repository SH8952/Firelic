Almost everyone building toward FIRE hits this fork in the road at some point: a bit of extra cash shows up at the end of the month, and it's unclear whether it should go toward a credit card balance, a student loan, or a brokerage account. Both directions feel productive, which is exactly why the decision stalls people out — without a clear comparison, it's easy to just do nothing, or to split it so thinly across both that neither option makes real progress. The good news is that this isn't actually a matter of opinion so much as arithmetic, once the numbers are laid out side by side.

## The "guaranteed return" math behind paying off debt first

Every dollar put toward a debt balance earns a return equal to that debt's interest rate, with no volatility and no uncertainty attached. Paying down a credit card sitting at 22% APR is, in effect, the same as finding an investment that is contractually guaranteed to return 22% a year — a rate no diversified stock portfolio has ever delivered reliably over time. That's the core reason most financial educators put high-interest debt near the very top of the priority list: **the comparison isn't debt versus stocks, it's a guaranteed 22% versus a historically-averaged, never-guaranteed 7-10%**. When the math is framed that way, extra cash going to a maxed-out card before it goes to an index fund stops looking conservative and starts looking like the higher-return choice.

## Where it gets less obvious: low-interest debt

The picture changes once the interest rate drops. A mortgage at 4%, a subsidized student loan at 3%, or an auto loan at 5% sits in a genuinely gray zone, because long-run stock market returns have historically landed somewhere in the 7-10% range before inflation — meaning investing instead of prepaying that debt has, on average, come out ahead, though never with certainty in any given year. This is where the decision starts depending on things a spreadsheet can't fully capture: how much the debt itself weighs on someone psychologically, whether the interest is tax-deductible in a given country's system, and how stable that person's income feels. There's no universally correct answer here — someone who loses sleep over any balance may rationally choose to prepay a 4% mortgage even knowing that investing has, historically, outperformed it slightly on average.

## A concrete example: two paths for the same $400 a month

Say someone earns roughly $50,000 a year, carries a $6,000 credit card balance at 22% APR, and separately has $15,000 left on a student loan at 5%. They find an extra $400 a month to put toward one or the other. Directing all $400 toward the credit card clears that balance in about 16 months and, along the way, avoids roughly $1,100 in interest charges that would otherwise have accrued — money that no market return needs to "beat," because it simply isn't spent. Only after that balance hits zero does it make sense to point the same $400 at the 5% student loan or into an investment account, at which point the comparison becomes far closer and reasonable people could land on either side depending on their own risk tolerance and timeline.

## The two exceptions almost everyone agrees on

Two situations tend to override the interest-rate math above, and it's worth checking for both before following the general rule. The first is an employer retirement match: if contributing to a workplace plan unlocks free matching money, capturing at least that match usually comes before extra debt payments, since walking away from a match is closer to leaving guaranteed money on the table than it is to a normal investing decision. The second is having no emergency fund at all — without at least a small cash buffer, an unexpected car repair or medical bill often ends up right back on the credit card that was just paid down, undoing the progress. Most people work through a rough order along the lines of: capture any employer match, build a small starter emergency fund, clear high-interest debt, build the emergency fund the rest of the way, then split additional money between remaining debt and investing based on the interest rate comparison above.

## Comparing common debt types side by side

Rather than a single rule for "debt," it helps to think through where different balances typically fall. Credit card and buy-now-pay-later balances usually carry rates well above any realistic investment return, which is why paying those off first has the strongest case behind it. Personal loans and auto loans sit in the middle, often in the 6-12% range, where prepaying still tends to make sense for most people but the gap versus investing is narrower. Mortgages and many subsidized student or government loans often sit at 3-5%, genuinely close enough to historical market averages that either choice — prepaying or investing the difference — is defensible, and the right pick often comes down to a person's comfort with carrying debt rather than a clear mathematical winner. None of this is a recommendation to take on or avoid any specific loan product; actual rates, terms, and any tax treatment vary by lender and country, so it's worth checking the details of an individual's own debt before treating any of these ranges as a rule.

## Common mistakes people make in this decision

The most common mistake is treating this as an all-or-nothing choice rather than a sequence — someone who could clear a high-interest card in eight months by pausing all investing often assumes they have to choose one path forever, when in reality the smart move is usually "debt first, then investing," not "debt only." A second mistake is ignoring the emergency fund step entirely and paying off debt so aggressively that any surprise expense goes right back onto a card, creating a cycle that never actually resolves. A third is comparing the wrong numbers — stacking a debt's stated interest rate against a single great year in the market, rather than against a realistic long-run average, which makes investing look like the obviously better choice when it usually isn't once high-interest debt is still on the table.
