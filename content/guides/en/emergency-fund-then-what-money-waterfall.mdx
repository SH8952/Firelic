Getting a first emergency fund in place feels like a finish line, but it's really just the first gate. The much more common question that follows is what to do with the next $200, $500, or $1,000 that shows up at the end of a month once that cushion exists — pay down a credit card, max out a workplace retirement match, keep building the cash buffer, or send it straight into a brokerage account. Without some kind of order to follow, it's easy to spread that money thin across all four and make slow progress on everything at once, which is usually worse than doing one thing at a time in the right sequence.

## Why the order matters more than the amount

Two people saving the exact same $400 a month can end up in very different financial positions a few years later, purely because of the order they followed. Money sent toward low-yield savings while a 24% APR credit card balance sits untouched is quietly losing far more to interest than it could ever earn sitting in a savings account, while money poured into a taxable brokerage account before an employer match is captured is money left on the table for good — a missed match doesn't come back next year. The waterfall framework exists precisely because these steps aren't interchangeable: each one closes off a specific kind of financial risk or captures a specific kind of free value, and skipping ahead usually means paying for it later.

## Step one: a starter cash buffer, before anything else

Before any of the later steps make sense, most FIRE planners suggest getting somewhere between one and three months of essential expenses into a basic savings account — not the full six-month fund most guides eventually recommend, just enough to absorb a flat tire or a smaller medical bill without reaching for a credit card. The reason this comes first, even ahead of an employer match in most cases, is that a genuinely empty cash cushion means any surprise expense gets financed at 20%+ credit card interest, which can erase months of progress made anywhere else in the waterfall.

## Step two: capture any employer match on the table

Once that starter buffer exists, the next stop for extra money is usually a workplace retirement plan, but only up to the point where an employer match stops. For example, if an employer matches 50% of contributions up to 6% of salary, contributing that 6% turns into an instant 50% return on that specific portion of money — a rate no stock, bond, or savings account can promise. Going past the matched amount at this stage isn't wrong, but it no longer carries that same guaranteed-return logic, which is why the waterfall treats "up to the match" and "beyond the match" as two separate decisions rather than one.

## Step three: high-interest debt versus finishing the emergency fund

This is the step where people most often get stuck, because it genuinely depends on the numbers involved rather than a fixed rule. A credit card or buy-now-pay-later balance sitting above roughly 15-20% interest is arguably a bigger financial emergency than an incomplete emergency fund, since that interest rate outpaces almost any realistic investment return; a lot of people find it reasonable to pause additional fund-building, clear that balance first, and then come back and finish building the fund up to the fuller three-to-six-month target. If there's no high-interest debt in the picture, the same money instead goes toward completing that fuller emergency fund before moving further down the waterfall.

## A concrete example: an extra $500 a month

Picture someone earning around $55,000 a year who has just built a one-month starter emergency fund and now has an extra $500 a month to direct. Their employer matches 50% up to 6% of salary, which works out to roughly $138 a month in contributions that unlock about $69 a month in free matching money — so that portion goes first. Suppose they're also carrying a $3,000 credit card balance at 23% APR; putting the remaining $362 a month toward that balance clears it in well under a year and avoids hundreds of dollars in interest that a savings account could never have outpaced. Only after that balance reaches zero does the same $362 a month move to finishing the emergency fund up to three months of expenses, and from there, into a diversified investment account. Someone with no high-interest debt at all would simply skip that middle step and move straight from the employer match into building out savings and investments.

## Where tax-advantaged accounts fit into the sequence

Retirement accounts with tax advantages — the specific names and rules vary widely by country, so it's worth checking what applies locally — often slot in somewhere around step three or four of this sequence, generally after high-interest debt is cleared but sometimes before a taxable brokerage account, depending on contribution limits and how the tax treatment compares to an individual's expected future bracket. There isn't a single ordering that fits everyone here; someone expecting a lower tax bracket in early retirement might prioritize tax-advantaged accounts more heavily, while someone who values flexibility to access funds before a traditional retirement age might lean more toward a taxable account earlier. This is a place where a rough framework is genuinely more useful than a rigid rule, since personal circumstances shift the right answer more than in the earlier steps.

## Common mistakes that break the waterfall

The most frequent mistake is running every step in parallel — splitting $400 four ways instead of following the sequence — which usually means the credit card balance lingers for years while a savings account earns a small fraction of what that balance is costing in interest. A second common mistake is stopping emergency fund contributions the moment a market dip makes investing feel urgent, only to have an unrelated car repair a month later force a withdrawal at the worst possible time. A third is treating the match percentage as optional information rather than the first thing to calculate — it's easy to underestimate how much free money is actually being missed until the number is written down. None of this is a suggestion to follow one exact order regardless of context; someone with unstable income, for instance, may reasonably want a larger buffer before moving further down the list at all.

Once a rough order like this is chosen, the FIRE Calculator above is a useful place to see what following it actually does to a timeline.
