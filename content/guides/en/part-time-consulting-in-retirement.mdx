Reaching your FIRE number doesn't automatically mean you stop earning money the next day. A large share of people who retire early keep doing some kind of paid work afterward — consulting in their old field, freelancing a few hours a week, or taking on project-based gigs — not because their plan failed, but because the math and the psychology of early retirement both tend to favor it. This guide looks at why that pattern is so common, and how to structure that income so it actually helps rather than quietly recreating the job you left.

## Why So Many FIRE Retirees Keep Working Somewhat

The instinct once you've hit your number is to assume the goal was to stop working entirely, but for most people who reach financial independence, the actual goal was control over whether and how they work, not the absence of income. Consulting or part-time work after FIRE typically pays disproportionately well relative to the hours involved, because you're selling deep experience without needing benefits, career progression, or full-time availability from the employer's side. A former engineering manager might earn $150 an hour doing 10 hours a week of technical advising — $75,000 a year for roughly a quarter of a full-time schedule. That income doesn't need to replace your full budget to matter; it just needs to reduce how much your portfolio has to cover.

## The Math: How a Little Income Changes a Lot

The effect of even modest post-retirement earnings compounds because of how withdrawal math works. If your annual spending is $50,000 and your portfolio was sized for a full 4% withdrawal rate, that requires roughly $1,250,000. If part-time consulting reliably brings in $15,000 a year, your portfolio only needs to cover the remaining $35,000 of spending, which drops the effective target to about $875,000 — a meaningful reduction, and one that also lowers the number of years you needed to work to get there in the first place. Beyond the static math, any year in which you don't have to sell investments to cover spending is also a year you're not exposed to sequence-of-returns risk, which is often a bigger threat to an early retirement than the raw withdrawal rate itself. The FIRE Calculator above can model this directly: run your numbers once with your full annual spending, then again with spending reduced by your expected part-time or consulting income, to see how much that flexibility actually moves your required portfolio size.

## Structuring the Work So It Doesn't Become a Second Full-Time Job

The risk with post-FIRE consulting isn't that it exists — it's that it quietly expands back into something resembling the job you left. Setting an explicit hours cap before taking on any client or project, and treating that cap as a hard boundary rather than a target, is the single most effective way to prevent this. Charging a higher hourly or project rate than you did as an employee is usually the right move too, both because your time is now genuinely more valuable to you and because a higher rate naturally filters for clients who respect your time rather than trying to fill your calendar. Many retirees also find it useful to keep the total number of active clients or commitments small — one or two ongoing relationships rather than a portfolio of five, since the cost of context-switching between clients tends to be underestimated until you're living it.

## Tax and Structural Considerations

Consulting or freelance income after early retirement is usually taxed differently than the employment income you had before, and the details vary significantly by country, so it's worth confirming the applicable rules with a tax professional or official guidance rather than assuming your old paycheck withholding logic still applies. In many places, self-employment or freelance income triggers separate tax categories, different social-insurance contribution rules, and sometimes new registration requirements, and some countries also treat this income differently depending on whether it's structured as a sole proprietorship, a small company, or straightforward freelance invoicing. This is also the point at which it's worth checking whether continuing to earn any income affects eligibility for benefits you were counting on, such as subsidized health coverage tied to income thresholds — a genuinely common surprise for early retirees in several countries.

## The Common Mistake: Letting "A Little Work" Creep Back Into a Career

The most common misstep isn't taking on too little post-retirement work — it's taking on too much, gradually, without noticing. A single client project stretches into a semi-regular engagement, then a second client appears, and eighteen months later the retiree is working close to full-time again, just without the title or the benefits that came with the old job. The fix isn't to avoid post-FIRE work altogether, since the financial and psychological upside is real for most people who try it; it's to revisit the arrangement on a fixed schedule — every six months is reasonable — and explicitly ask whether the current workload still matches the amount of freedom you set out to buy in the first place. If a client relationship has grown past that comfortable ceiling, it's worth actively renegotiating the scope or the rate rather than letting it drift.

## Putting This Into Your Plan

If you're weighing whether to build some part-time or consulting income into your retirement plan, the most useful exercise is running your numbers both ways in the FIRE Calculator above: once assuming zero post-retirement income, and once assuming a conservative, realistic estimate of what part-time work in your field could bring in. Comparing the two required portfolio sizes and the two timelines to get there gives you an honest picture of how much that flexibility is actually worth, and lets you decide deliberately — rather than by accident — how much continued work, if any, belongs in your version of financial independence.
