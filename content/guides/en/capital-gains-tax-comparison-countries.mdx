Once you're financially independent, most of your annual income no longer comes from a paycheck — it comes from selling investments, and in most countries that triggers capital gains tax. The rate you actually pay on that gain can vary enormously depending on where you live, how long you held the investment, and how your local system is structured, which makes this one of the few areas where your FIRE number can shift meaningfully based on geography alone, even before you touch the investment side of the plan.

## Why Capital Gains Tax Rules Vary So Much by Country

Every country treats investment gains a little differently, and there's no single global standard to compare against. Some tax systems apply one flat rate to any capital gain regardless of income level, others fold the gain into your regular taxable income and tax it at progressive rates, and a handful of countries don't tax personal capital gains on most investments at all. On top of that base structure, many countries add distinctions for how long you held the asset, whether it was held inside a tax-advantaged retirement account, and whether you're a tax resident there in the first place. Because these rules change over time and depend on your specific circumstances, this guide describes the general shapes these systems take rather than quoting exact rates — you'll want to confirm the current rules for your own country (and, if you're planning an international move, your destination country) with an official source or a qualified tax professional before building a plan around them.

## Three Common Approaches: Flat Rate, Progressive Rate, and No Tax at All

Broadly speaking, capital gains systems tend to fall into one of three shapes. A flat-rate system applies a single percentage to gains no matter how much you earn otherwise, which tends to make tax bills more predictable for early retirees living on modest withdrawals. A progressive system treats gains as part of your total taxable income for the year, which can actually work in a FIRE retiree's favor if your other income is low, since a large chunk of the gain may fall into a lower bracket than it would have while you were working full-time — though it can also mean a single large gain in one year pushes you into a higher bracket than you expected. A small number of jurisdictions don't tax capital gains on personal investment portfolios at all, which is one reason some FIRE planners factor potential relocation into their long-term strategy, though it's worth remembering that citizenship-based tax obligations, exit taxes, and other rules can complicate that idea depending on where you're a citizen or resident today.

## A Worked Example: Same Portfolio, Different Tax Outcomes

To see why this matters in practice, imagine three hypothetical retirees who each sell $40,000 worth of long-held index fund shares in a year, with $15,000 of that amount representing the actual gain (the rest being their original cost basis, which generally isn't taxed again). In a country with a progressive system and a low-income tax bracket that FIRE retirees often land in, a meaningful share of that $15,000 gain might be taxed lightly or not at all. In a country with a flat capital gains rate, the same $15,000 gain would generally be taxed at that flat percentage regardless of how little other income the retiree has that year. In a country with no personal capital gains tax, the full $15,000 would typically stay in the retiree's pocket. None of these outcomes are “better” in the abstract — they just illustrate how the same withdrawal, from the same portfolio, can produce noticeably different after-tax spending money depending purely on tax jurisdiction, which is exactly why this deserves its own line item in a FIRE plan rather than being treated as a rounding error.

## Holding Period, Residency, and Other Rules That Change the Math

Beyond the basic rate structure, a few recurring variables tend to matter across most countries. Many systems apply a lower rate, or an exemption, to assets held longer than some minimum period (often around a year), which rewards the long-term, buy-and-hold approach that most FIRE portfolios already use rather than requiring any special planning. Tax residency status is another major factor: some countries tax based on where you legally reside for a set number of days per year, while others tax citizens on worldwide income regardless of where they currently live, which is an important distinction to research carefully if part of your FIRE plan involves living abroad for part or all of the year. Retirement-account wrappers — the kind covered in country-specific guides on this site — often change the picture further, since gains inside those accounts are frequently taxed differently (or not at all until withdrawal) compared to a regular taxable brokerage account.

## Common Mistakes FIRE Planners Make With Capital Gains Tax

The most common mistake is building a FIRE number entirely around gross withdrawal amounts and never modeling what the after-tax number actually looks like, which can leave a meaningful gap between the plan on paper and the spending money that actually shows up. A second common mistake is assuming tax rules will stay exactly as they are today for the next 20 or 30 years of retirement — a reasonable planning assumption in the short term, but one that generally deserves a buffer given how frequently governments adjust these thresholds and rates. A third is only checking one country's rules for a plan that involves moving internationally, without accounting for how the home country and destination country's rules interact (some countries have tax treaties that reduce double taxation; others don't, or only partially do).

## What This Means If You Might Retire Across Borders

If part of your FIRE plan involves spending years in a different country than the one you currently live in, capital gains treatment is genuinely worth researching early rather than after you've already relocated, since exit taxes, residency triggers, and treaty rules can be complex and sometimes irreversible once you've acted. This isn't a reason to avoid international plans — many FIRE retirees do live and travel abroad successfully — but it is a reason to treat "which country taxes my withdrawals, and how" as its own research project alongside the more commonly discussed questions like healthcare access and visa requirements, ideally with guidance from a tax professional familiar with both jurisdictions involved.

## Using the FIRE Calculator to Plan Around Taxes

The calculator above works from the spending number you enter, so the most useful way to account for capital gains tax is to build a tax-adjusted version of your expected annual withdrawal before you plug it in: instead of entering your gross target spending, estimate what you'd actually owe in capital gains tax on a typical year's withdrawals under your country's rules, and enter your net, after-tax spending need as your annual expenses figure instead. Running the calculator once with an optimistic (low-tax) assumption and once with a more conservative (higher-tax) assumption gives you a realistic range for your FIRE number rather than a single figure that quietly assumes taxes away.
